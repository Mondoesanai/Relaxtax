Who Can Represent You With the IRS?

2026-10-17

If the IRS sends you a letter, schedules an audit, or you owe back taxes, you might assume anyone who "knows taxes" can step in and handle it for you. That's not quite true. The IRS only allows certain people to represent you directly, and knowing the difference can save you time and stress.

Who Is Actually Allowed to Represent You?

The IRS has specific rules about who can speak and act on your behalf. Generally, representation rights are limited to:

  • Enrolled Agents (EAs) — federally licensed tax professionals authorized to represent taxpayers before the IRS
  • Certified Public Accountants (CPAs) — licensed accountants with representation rights
  • Attorneys — particularly those with tax law experience

These professionals can communicate with the IRS for you, attend meetings on your behalf, and negotiate directly with agents. A regular tax preparer without one of these credentials typically cannot represent you in an audit or dispute, even if they prepared your return.

Why This Distinction Matters

If you're ever audited or receive a notice about owed taxes, having someone who actually has representation rights means they can step in fully — not just advise you from the sidelines. This is especially important if the situation involves back taxes, penalties, or a dispute over what you owe.

It's worth asking any tax professional upfront whether they're authorized to represent you if things get complicated, not just prepare your return.

What IRS Representation Can Involve

Depending on your situation, representation might include:

  • Responding to IRS notices or letters
  • Attending audit meetings on your behalf
  • Negotiating payment arrangements for back taxes
  • Communicating with the IRS so you don't have to

    • Having someone handle this on your behalf can take a lot of the stress out of a process that often feels confusing and intimidating.

    What to Do If You Get an IRS Notice

    If you receive a letter from the IRS, don't ignore it — but don't panic either. The first step is figuring out exactly what the notice is asking for and whether you need professional representation or just need to respond with information.

    A tax professional with representation rights can review the notice, explain what it means in plain language, and tell you what (if anything) needs to happen next.
